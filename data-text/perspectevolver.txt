Introduction

Developing a research idea requires exploring a vast hypothesis space and refining promising possibilities into hypotheses

worth pursuing [39, 43]. Colleagues with different perspectives broaden this exploration by exposing assumptions and

introducing possibilities researchers may not consider independently [1, 16, 43]. Those perspectives can also change

as new distinctions, explanations, or evidence revise how researchers understand the problem and what they pursue

next [25].

LLM-based multi-agent systems increasingly support collaborative ideation by assigning agents different perspec-

tives [29–31, 40]. These perspectives expose researchers to alternative interpretations and can broaden the ideas

considered during research ideation [29–31, 40].

However, prior multi-agent systems primarily support configuring diverse agent perspectives, with less support

for understanding how those perspectives contribute to and change as the research direction develops [29, 30, 40].

Researchers must reassess the developing research direction, including which perspectives remain relevant, how

they have changed, and which questions warrant further investigation. Supporting this ongoing sensemaking around

perspective configuration, contribution, and evolution remains a design challenge for multi-agent research tools.

To understand how researchers manage perspectives in AI-supported ideation, we conducted a formative study with

six researchers using general-purpose LLMs in their existing workflows. We found three challenges: (1) distinguishing

what each perspective represents, (2) understanding how each shapes the developing research direction, and (3)

determining how those perspectives should change as that direction develops. Researchers had to reconstruct and
update this context manually across interactions.

Building on these findings, we developed PerspectEvolver (Figure 1), an interactive multi-agent system that externalizes

literature-grounded Perspectives as inspectable and revisable representations. Perspective Formation organizes relevant

literature into regions and forms Perspectives for researchers to assemble into a Perspective Ensemble. Perspective-

Guided Deliberation develops research directions through Deliberative Threads and consolidates each discussion into a

Working Synthesis. Perspective Evolution uses these syntheses to revise Perspectives and the Investigation Brief and

generate follow-up questions.

We evaluated PerspectEvolver in a within-subjects study with 10 participants from bioengineering, computer science,

health sciences, electrical and computer engineering, and mechanical engineering. We compared PerspectEvolver against

a group-chat multi-agent baseline that retained literature retrieval, multi-agent deliberation, and hypothesis editing, but

follow-up question generation. We analyzed system logs, survey responses, think-aloud data, interviews, and changes

Our work contributes to HCI research on multi-agent systems for scientific ideation in three ways:

• Design and implementation of PerspectEvolver. We introduce an interactive multi-agent system that

externalizes literature-grounded Perspectives as inspectable and revisable representations. Researchers form

Perspectives from the literature, deliberate with them through Perspective Agents, and revise them as the

investigation develops. Deliberative Threads and Working Syntheses preserve how interpretations develop and

inform subsequent Perspective revision and inquiry.

• Empirical evidence from a within-subjects study. Participants using PerspectEvolver developed hypotheses

with significantly greater improvements in novelty, while plausibility, significance, and testability also improved

more on average but did not differ significantly from the baseline. They also rated PerspectEvolver higher in

usability and in its support for divergent and convergent thinking, while reporting significantly lower mental

demand and effort. Prompt analyses further showed that interactions with PerspectEvolver focused more on

advancing the hypothesis through generation, exploration, synthesis, and selection, whereas baseline

interactions focused more on establishing the grounds for those moves.

• Design implications for perspective-based multi-agent systems. We identify three design implications:

maintaining the context and provenance needed to ground research decisions while preserving researcher

judgment over how the inquiry advances; providing an outlet for unresolved disagreement through subsequent

exploration; and expanding perspective diversity without proportionally increasing cognitive load.

Related work

We review prior work on how perspectives evolve through collaboration and why that evolution requires sensemaking

(§2.1), on how multi-agent ideation systems configure and evolve the perspectives they assign to agents (§2.2), and on

the divergent and convergent activities that perspective evolution sustains (§2.3).

2.1

Perspective Evolution in Collaborative Hypothesis Generation

Research ideation is an iterative, non-linear process in which ideas emerge, develop, and take new directions as

researchers encounter new information and interpretations [39, 43]. As researchers develop hypotheses, they interpret

relevant literature through their current understanding of the problem, while new findings can in turn change that

understanding [17, 25]. Klein’s data–frame account captures this reciprocal process: a frame guides which information

researchers attend to and how they interpret it, while incoming data can prompt them to question and revise that

frame [25]. Hypothesis generation therefore depends on both the evidence available and the perspective through which

researchers interpret it. Collaboration introduces additional perspectives into this process. Colleagues may approach the

same problem with different assumptions, explanations, and criteria, drawing attention to relationships or limitations

that are less visible from a single framing [43]. Working through these differences can change both the hypotheses

researchers develop and the perspectives through which they understand the problem. As perspectives evolve, they

influence what researchers consider next, creating new starting points for further ideation.

First, collaboration reveals differences in how researchers understand and develop an initial research direction. When

a researcher brings a tentative direction to colleagues, they may reach different conclusions because they rely on

different assumptions, explanations, and criteria [1]. These differences can introduce alternative ways of understanding

the problem and broaden the directions considered during hypothesis generation [16].

Second, researchers work through these differences through deliberation, examining where their perspectives conflict,

where they align, and whether and how the developing hypothesis should change [41]. Disagreements identify questions

that remain unresolved, while areas of agreement establish a shared basis for further reasoning. In this way, deliberation

clarifies differences among perspectives while bringing together insights that can refine the developing hypothesis.

Third, deliberation can change the perspectives themselves. Counterarguments can qualify earlier assumptions, new

evidence can change how researchers interpret the problem, and alternative explanations can become part of their

perspective [5]. These revised perspectives then shape how researchers interpret subsequent information and which

directions they consider next [35]. The researchers’ perspectives, therefore, develop alongside the hypotheses they
explore.

2.2

Configuring and Evolving Perspectives in Multi-Agent Ideation Systems

Recent LLM-based multi-agent systems support ideation by assigning agents distinct personas that expose users to

different perspectives on a problem [6, 29–31, 40]. Across these systems, agent perspectives are configured through

two broad approaches: role-based personas and domain-based personas. Role-based systems differentiate agents by

assigning functional, occupational, or genre roles that shape how each contributes to the task. MultiColleagues represents

occupation-based personas as colleagues that interact in explore and focus modes [40]. CritiqueCrew assigns UX,

product, and engineering roles to provide complementary design critiques, which a lead coordinator synthesizes and

compares [6]. NarrativeLoom uses ten genre-specific personas to generate alternative story beats in parallel for users to

review and select [31]. Domain-based systems differentiate agents by the research areas or expertise they represent.

PersonaFlow constructs domain-expert personas from the research question, retrieved literature, or profiles of relevant

authors, representing each through a role and associated expertise [30]. Perspectra selects personas from a catalog

organized around research areas, focus areas, and methodological skills [29].

Configuration divides into two approaches. Role-based systems assign agents fixed functional or genre roles—

colleagues in MultiColleagues [40], UX, product, and engineering reviewers in CritiqueCrew [6], genre storytellers

in NarrativeLoom [31]. Domain-based systems derive agents from research areas instead: PersonaFlow synthesizes

domain-expert personas from the research question or from retrieved papers and their authors [30], and Perspectra

draws agents from a catalog built by clustering a taxonomy of research areas and methodological skills [29].

In systems where agents deliberate with one another, attribution specifies the speaker of each message and thereby
identifies the perspective to which that message belongs [6, 29, 31]. MultiColleagues, for instance, gives each persona

a distinct profile image and role-based message styling so that contributions remain identifiable across an extended

discussion; participants reported that anchoring contributions to individual colleagues made it easier to follow up

on an idea and to combine ideas into coherent outcomes [? ]. Attribution alone, however, requires the researcher to

reconstruct how a given perspective contributed to the developing discussion: what it supported or challenged, whether
its contribution was taken up or revised, and which disagreements remained unresolved.

Support for perspective evolution is more limited. In MultiColleagues, CritiqueCrew, NarrativeLoom, and PersonaFlow,

agents retain their initially configured personas throughout the interaction; changing a persona requires separate

user intervention [6, 30, 31, 40]. The discussion may introduce new ideas or disagreements, but the agent does not

incorporate them into its perspective in later interactions. Perspectra provides partial support for this evolution through

long-term memory, which periodically distills an agent’s previous contributions, retrieved literature, and challenges

from other agents into research-idea snippets that inform its subsequent reasoning [29]. However, the system does not

surface which parts of the deliberation motivated a particular change in the agent’s perspective.

Taken together, prior systems provide mechanisms for introducing diverse agent perspectives, but offer less support

for understanding how those perspectives develop through deliberation. Attribution can identify which agent produced

a response, and Perspectra allows information from prior interaction to inform later reasoning, yet the relationship

between what a perspective contributes and how it subsequently changes remains largely implicit. Researchers therefore

have limited support for understanding how a perspective is configured, how it contributes during deliberation, and

whether and how it changes through that deliberation [38].

PerspectEvolver addresses this gap by making the development of Perspectives inspectable across formation, deliberation, and evolution. Perspective Formation forms literature-grounded Perspectives so researchers can examine

what informs each interpretation. Perspective-Guided Deliberation makes each Perspective’s contributions visible during

deliberation and shows how those contributions affect the developing discussion. Perspective Evolution makes subse-

quent Perspective changes inspectable by showing what changed, why it changed, and which discussion prompted it.
Together, these phases support researchers in understanding how Perspectives are formed, how they contribute during
deliberation, and how they develop through that deliberation.

2.3

Divergent and Convergent Thinking Activities in Research Ideation

Scientific hypothesis generation is an iterative process of reasoning, exploring a vast hypothesis space and refining

hypothesis candidates [39, 43]. Divergent thinking widens the region of the hypothesis space a researcher explores,

pushing exploration past what they already know; Schunn and Klahr [44] observe that exhaustively searching available

datasets surfaces insightful features precisely because it works through scientific facts thoroughly. Convergent

thinking then narrows that space, weighing candidates against multiple criteria, and Dunbar [10] argues that verifying

and focusing on particular data types or characteristics is indispensable to scientific discovery. Iteration of both modes,

repeating cycles of divergence and convergence, iteratively filters the expanded space until a small set of dependable

candidates remains. As Sawyer [42] emphasizes, a creative idea does not develop linearly within either mode, but through

a non-linear process in which the two alternate again and again. Supporting both modes, along with the movement

between them, is what enables researchers to arrive at hypotheses they can hold with confidence [24, 37, 43, 44].

Researchers’ work in this alternation divides into two kinds, which we call advancing and grounding. Advancing

changes the state of the working hypothesis: a candidate is introduced, two are merged, or one is kept and the rest set

aside. Grounding leaves that state intact and brings existing material to bear on it—prior work, established criteria,

or the researcher’s own knowledge. Both are essential for developing promising hypotheses, but they contribute

differently: grounding shapes how candidates are judged, while advancing is what changes them. Introducing, merging,

and committing to candidates each leave the working hypothesis in a different state, and the next round of deliberation

builds on that updated state [42]. Through this repeated updating, the region of the hypothesis space a researcher

covers widens, and a promising hypothesis emerges within that region [16, 44].

In this work, we explore designs that nudge participants to engage in divergent and convergent thinking through

sensemaking over perspectives and deliberation. More specifically, we design perspective-guided deliberation in which

users interact with diverse perspectives through activities such as synthesis, integration, reflection, and exploration, so

that the interaction itself provides grounding and researchers can focus on advancing the working hypothesis.

Formative study

The formative study aimed to understand (1) how researchers collaborate with colleagues to develop scientific hypotheses;

(2) how researchers turn to AI as an alternative when collaboration with colleagues is not feasible; and (3) the limitations

and opportunities of existing AI systems for supporting hypothesis development, which informed our design goals for

a multi-agent system.

3.1

Participants

We recruited six participants: three postdoctoral researchers and three PhD candidates from bioengineering, electrical

and computer engineering, mechanical engineering, and chemical engineering (Table 2). Participants had 4–8 years of

research experience (𝑀 = 5.5, 𝑆𝐷 = 1.87). We recruited participants through personal referrals and outreach to research

labs. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the study, and all participants provided informed consent. Each

participant received a $30 gift certificate. When reporting interview quotes, we use 𝑃𝑛 to denote the participant number.

3.2

Study Procedure

The study consisted of three parts. First, we conducted a 30-minute in-depth interview covering (1) participants’

workflow in collaboration for hypothesis ideation, (2) their goals and strategies for using AI tools when collaborating

with colleagues is not feasible, and (3) the challenges and limitations they encounter when using AI tools for hypothesis

development. Because participants had no prior experience with multi-agent systems, we focused on their use of

existing AI tools. Next, we observed participants for 20 minutes as they developed a hypothesis for a research problem

from their ongoing work. Participants used their usual general-purpose LLM service (ChatGPT for all six) as they

normally would, prompting it to adopt perspectives relevant to the research problem. Finally, a 30-minute post-task

interview examined participants’ refinement strategies, challenges, and desired support. Each session lasted 1.5 hours.

We analyzed the interview transcripts using thematic analysis and iterative open coding [8]. Two authors coded the

transcripts for emerging themes, which the co-authors discussed until reaching consensus.

3.3

Findings

3.3.1 Collaboration for Hypothesis Generation. We found that collaborative hypothesis development involved three

recurring stages: (1) formulating an initial hypothesis from domain literature, (2) engaging with collaborators who held

different perspectives, and (3) evolving perspectives and refining hypotheses around unresolved issues. First, researchers

formulated an initial hypothesis by interpreting domain literature through their own perspective. They reviewed prior

work to identify findings, mechanisms, and unexplored areas, then organized them around questions aligned with their

interests and perspectives. As 𝑃6 described, “I start with papers that align with my interests and perspectives, particularly

work by researchers whose perspectives are similar to my own. I also look through papers that I have already identified or

listed, tracing what has been tried and what has not, until the hypothesis feels sharp enough.” Next, researchers engaged
with collaborators who held different perspectives. These collaborators introduced alternative viewpoints that revealed

blind spots in the researchers’ initial perspectives. This prompts researchers to reconsider and expand their perspectives.

As 𝑃1 described “During our meeting, other researchers challenged my perspective on the differentiation timeline, asking

if the early-stage cell population was too heterogeneous. That feedback made me reconsider my assumptions about the

timing of differentiation and scrutinize our flow cytometry data from scratch.” Finally, researchers used unresolved issues

from earlier discussions to initiate further rounds, evolving their perspectives and refining their hypotheses through

continued collaboration. For example, 𝑃 3 described “After a few months working with the control engineers, their pushback

on the hardware side stopped feeling like a roadblock. I started reading it as a constraint I just hadn’t accounted for yet.

Once I could think in their terms too, the hypothesis got a lot sharper.”

This collaborative process typically unfolds over several weeks to months before experimental validation. However,

collaboration faced practical barriers from limited professional networks, scheduling constraints, and discomfort

receiving critical feedback. For example, “At first, I found their questions difficult because they felt like challenges to

myself. It was uncomfortable to share an idea that was still incomplete.” (𝑃5 ). “The collaborators I can work with are largely

limited by my own network. Also, it is often difficult to meet regularly because of our individual schedules.” (𝑃2 ). As a result,

researchers used AI as an accessible alternative for exploring and refining hypotheses.

3.3.2 Roles of AI in Research Ideation. Although prior multi-agent systems support diverse agent perspectives, none

of our participants had prior experience with such systems. Instead, they relied on general-purpose AI services

such as ChatGPT1 for individual hypothesis ideation. To simulate collaboration, participants constructed alternative

perspectives, deliberated with them, and updated them as discussions developed. First, participants constructed an

alternative perspective by uploading papers with views different from or opposing their own and providing instructions

to contextualize that perspective.

As 𝑃4 described, “When I was thinking about how to improve AI accuracy, I uploaded papers that focused more on

efficiency, because that was a somewhat different perspective from my own. I wanted the LLM to help me compare these

perspectives and find where they could be connected.”

Second, participants deliberated with these perspectives, either introducing them into an existing conversation
or using separate conversations to keep their responses independent. Participants then summarized the discussions

and compared their outputs to identify converging and conflicting points. As 𝑃5 described, “Sometimes I bring another

perspective into the same conversation, but when I want to keep the opinions separate, I start a new chat. Then I look

at the summary or at what each perspective said, and work out how to develop my hypothesis from there.” Participants

also reconsidered how they framed the research problem in response to different perspectives. As 𝑃 2 described, “I was

3.3.3 Key Challenges in the Current Workflow. Our workflow observations revealed three challenges in configuring,
deliberating with, and updating AI perspectives.

First, participants had no structured way to specify how an agent should interpret uploaded papers, leaving its

stance, assumptions, and evidentiary standards opaque and sometimes misaligned with their intent. As 𝑃1 described, “I

gave it three of Baker’s papers and said ‘think like this,’ but I couldn’t tell what it actually took from them. It just sounded

like a generic biologist.” 𝑃2 described a related difficulty distinguishing multiple perspectives: “I would set up several

perspectives, but it was hard to tell clearly what made them different. The more the discussion went on, the stronger that

ambiguity got.”

Second, participants had difficulty understanding how each perspective shaped the discussion. Even when the
discussion reached an agreement or exposed a disagreement, they could not tell what each perspective had contributed

to that result. As 𝑃2 described, “Using several agents was useful, but it was hard to know what each of them had contributed

to a given agreement.” This made it harder to judge how the discussion had arrived at its conclusions and which

perspective to involve next. 𝑃6 added, “An agreement would come out, but I couldn’t tell whether one side’s position had

been taken up or whether the two had met somewhere. So nothing came to mind about which perspective to add next.”

Third, participants had difficulty determining how discussion outcomes should revise each perspective. They

summarized the discussion, interpreted its implications themselves, and manually updated the corresponding perspective

prompts. As 𝑃3 described, “The agents sometimes drifted away from the new discussion and its context, so I updated their

perspectives myself. If I didn’t, the context seemed to get tangled later on.” These revisions were not tracked as part of the

discussion, leaving participants to remember what had changed and why across rounds. 𝑃4 also described uncertainty

about how to translate discussion insights into perspective updates: “Even when I pull an insight out of the deliberation,

I’m not sure how to reflect it. If I miss it, a gap seems to open between me and the perspectives later, so I go back and update

them again.”
3.3.4 Elements of a Perspective. Our findings showed that participants often defined AI perspectives indirectly through
papers and instructions, making it difficult to understand what distinguished one perspective from another. We

therefore asked participants to examine their own research papers and identify the elements that distinguished different

perspectives. Drawing on prior work on framing [11], we used two higher-level concepts as a starting point: Framing,

how a perspective understands the research problem, and Position, where it stands within that framing. From these

interviews, we identified four foundational elements, which we represent as Perspective Fragments: Scope, Explanation,

Approach, and Significance. These fragments characterize different aspects of a perspective and can inform both its

Framing and Position.

3.4

Design Goals

Based on these findings, we proposed three design goals for our system:

• DG1. Representing a perspective as inspectable elements. When configuring perspectives for multiple

agents, the interface should make explicit what each perspective takes from the literature, so that users can tell

one perspective from another.

• DG2. Surfacing each perspective’s contribution to the deliberation. When several perspectives deliberate,

the interface should record what each perspective contributed across the deliberations, so that users can judge
how far those outcomes can be trusted without rereading the exchanges.

• DG3. Carrying what the deliberation settled into the perspective evolution and the hypothesis. When

a deliberation concludes, the interface should make explicit how its outcome revises each perspective and the

hypothesis, and leave what remains open as the starting point for the round that follows.

System and Implementation

Building on these design goals (DG1–3), we present PerspectEvolver, an interactive multi-agent system for research

ideation through deliberation among diverse Perspectives. Each Perspective is grounded in an area of scientific literature

and represents how research in that area frames, explains, and investigates the research problem. Researchers select

Perspectives to form a Perspective Ensemble. Within the Ensemble, PerspectEvolver externalizes each Perspective as a

Perspective Agent that contributes its interpretation and supporting evidence during deliberation.

PerspectEvolver organizes this process into three phases. Perspective Formation supports researchers in exploring the

literature and forming an initial Perspective Ensemble. Perspective-Guided Deliberation develops research directions

through Proposals, Deliberative Threads, and Working Syntheses. Perspective Evolution refines affected Perspectives,

proposes Investigation Brief revisions, and generates follow-up questions from synthesized discussions. Additional

technical details appear in Appendix A.4.

4.1

Perspective Formation: Exploring and Forming Perspectives (DG1)

Perspective Formation supports researchers in exploring alternative interpretations of a research problem by forming

Perspectives grounded in relevant literature (Figure 2). Elicitation establishes the Investigation Brief, Discovery retrieves

and organizes relevant literature, and Formation constructs candidate Perspectives for researchers to assemble into a
Perspective Ensemble.

Methodology, and Expected Results (Figure 2-➀). The brief remains visible and editable throughout the investigation

(Figure 4, left), recording the researcher’s problem framing and intended study.

4.1.2 Discovery. From the Investigation Brief, PerspectEvolver generates research directions and translates them into

abstracts, and body text, allowing retrieval through findings, methods, or arguments not prominent in titles or abstracts.

Retrieved passages can prompt additional searches for topics not covered by the initial queries.

The system obtains metadata and SPECTER2 embeddings [46] for retrieved papers and organizes them into literature

regions using UMAP [33] for dimensionality reduction and HDBSCAN [32] for density-based clustering. The Literature

Map presents papers within these labeled regions for researchers to inspect and compare (Figure 2-➁). Papers in each

region provide source material for a candidate Perspective.

4.1.3 Formation. From each literature region, PerspectEvolver forms a candidate Perspective representing one interpre-

tation of the research problem. Each Perspective includes a Framing that describes how it understands the problem and

a Position that expresses its conclusions, qualifications, uncertainties, or proposed directions. Perspective Fragments

characterize its Scope, Explanation, Approach, and Significance. Each field references the Literature Evidence used to

form it, with each evidence item linked to its source passage.

Candidate Perspectives appear as Perspective Cards alongside the Literature Map (Figure 2-➂). Researchers compare

the candidate interpretations and inspect their supporting sources before selecting two or three to form a Perspective

Ensemble. Choosing Create panel begins deliberation with the selected Perspective Agents.

4.2

Perspective-Guided Deliberation: Thread-based Deliberation (DG2)

Perspective-Guided Deliberation develops research directions through Proposals, Deliberative Threads, and Working

Syntheses (Figure 3-➀–➁). Proposals introduce directions for inquiry, Deliberative Threads develop them through

discussion among Perspective Agents, and Working Syntheses record discussion outcomes at points chosen by the

researcher.

4.2.1 Proposals. Each selected Perspective introduces a Proposal comprising a research question, tentative hypothesis,

and rationale. Researchers Keep or Skip each Proposal and can Undo either decision. Confirming retained Proposals

creates a Deliberative Thread for each direction.

Each thread initially includes the selected Perspectives so that a Proposal from one can be examined through the

others’ interpretations and literature. Researchers can also introduce their own research question and choose which

Perspectives participate.

4.2.2 Deliberative Threads. Each Deliberative Thread organizes discussion around one research question. Perspective
Agents contribute interpretations and supporting evidence from their respective Perspectives. One Perspective may

challenge an explanation with contrary evidence, qualify it by introducing a relevant condition, or offer an alternative

explanation.

Threads are organized as Active or Open, with each card showing its research question, discussion activity, and
participating Perspectives (Figure 3-➀). The Canvas presents threads as branches from the investigation’s research

question, allowing researchers to move among research questions while seeing how their threads relate (Figure 4, right).

Active threads can continue to develop, while Open threads remain available for later investigation.

Selecting a thread opens its discussion. Each contribution identifies its Perspective and the earlier contribution or

Discuss requests one to six additional turns, while Stop interrupts the exchange. Researchers can reply to or @-mention

Expand asks for further explanation.

4.2.3 Working Synthesis. Selecting Synthesize produces a Working Synthesis of the discussion at that point. It con-

solidates findings, limitations, unresolved disagreements, and open questions. Compatible ideas are integrated while

unresolved differences remain explicit, and empirical findings retain links to supporting literature. When the discussion

motivates a testable hypothesis, the synthesis also records a tentative prediction and rationale.

The thread’s Artifacts view stores a Prediction, its Limitations, and an Evaluation Plan. On the Canvas, a synthesized

thread can expose its hypothesis, open questions, and follow-up directions within the investigation structure (Figure 4,

right). Researchers can return to a synthesized thread, question or extend its conclusions, and synthesize it again.

4.3

Perspective Evolution: Reflection, Refinement, and Emergent Threads (DG3)

Following synthesis, Perspective Evolution refines affected Perspectives, proposes Investigation Brief changes, and
generates follow-up questions. Reflection evaluates whether Discussion Inputs require changes to participating Perspec-

tives, while document refinement compares the Working Synthesis with the Investigation Brief (Figure 5). Unresolved

questions can become new Deliberative Threads for subsequent investigation.

4.3.1 Perspective Reflection. After synthesis, PerspectEvolver extracts Discussion Inputs that distinguish empirical

findings from scientific considerations and retain references to their supporting evidence and discussion sources.

Reflection compares these inputs with each participating Perspective and refines it only when an input contradicts or
qualifies an existing statement or resolves an expressed uncertainty.

Reflection is constrained to change only affected Perspective fields. When a Perspective changes, revision cards

compare its previous and revised content by field (Figure 3-➂). Researchers can inspect how the discussion refined a

Perspective rather than seeing only its current state. History records each revision with its previous and revised text,
Why this changed, and the Deliberative Thread that prompted it.

4.3.2 Document Refinement. From the same Working Synthesis, PerspectEvolver proposes revisions to affected sections

of the Investigation Brief (Figure 5). Each proposal shows additions and deletions within the affected section together

with the reason for the revision.

Researchers decide which changes enter the Investigation Brief through Accept, Edit, or Reject, then confirm them

with Apply decisions. This keeps changes to the research direction under researcher control. Researchers can also revise

the brief directly. Annotate attaches a note to selected text or lets the researcher @-mention a Perspective, while Rewrite

Anon.

previews a proposed replacement before application. Direct editing remains available throughout. The Document view

presents the revised Investigation Brief (Figure 4, left), which subsequent deliberation uses as context.

4.3.3 Emergent Threads. PerspectEvolver uses the Working Synthesis and current Investigation Brief to propose follow-

up questions from unresolved disagreements, limitations, and open questions. Suggestions appear under Suggested

Threads and as prospective Canvas branches (Figure 4, right) for researchers to review before opening them. Selecting

a suggestion opens a new Deliberative Thread linked to its originating discussion. Subsequent threads use the latest
versions of participating Perspectives.

4.4

Implementation

PerspectEvolver is implemented as a web application with a Next.js2 , React, and TypeScript frontend and a Python

backend. React Flow3 and Dagre4 implement the Canvas, D35 renders the Literature Map, and Tiptap6 supports document

editing and annotations. FastAPI7 exposes backend endpoints, PostgreSQL8 stores investigation and revision state, and

LangGraph9 coordinates checkpointed human-in-the-loop workflows.

DSPy [22] implements structured language-model operations. Perspective Agents share task-specific modules, which

receive the current Perspective, evidence, and discussion context as inputs. We use OpenAI’s gpt-5.6-luna and

gpt-5.6-terra models10 . gpt-5.6-luna supports search planning, evidence extraction, Perspective naming, proposal

generation, and interactive deliberation, while gpt-5.6-terra supports Perspective synthesis, Working Synthesis,

Reflection, document revision, and follow-up generation. Literature retrieval follows the Discovery pipeline described

in §4.1.2. Voyage rerankers11 select evidence during Formation and deliberation. Evidence extraction identifies scientific

claims and study details from retrieved passages and retains only items whose supporting quotations occur verbatim in

their mapped source passages. Perspective validation requires Literature Evidence references for every nonempty field.

Perspective Reflection compares Discussion Inputs with each Perspective and proposes refinements only to fields it

identifies as affected. Additional model, retrieval, agent, and workflow details appear in Appendix A.4.

5.1

Baseline

Based on our formative study, in which researchers used mainstream chat-based LLMs for hypothesis development,

we implemented a group-chat MAS baseline that preserved the core workflow of literature retrieval, multi-agent

deliberation, and hypothesis revision while omitting PerspectEvolver’s explicit perspective-sensemaking mechanisms.

Perspective Formation retained query recommendation and paper retrieval, but represented Perspectives through

simpler paper-grounded personas rather than literature clustering and structured Perspective extraction. Perspective-

Guided Deliberation retained multi-agent deliberation, suggested topics, and discussion summaries, but did not explicitly

surface how each Perspective contributed to the discussion. The document-refinement workflow retained the hypothesis

document and transfer of discussion outcomes, but did not support explicit Perspective evolution or follow-up question

generation.

5.2

Study Procedures

We conducted a within-subjects study with 10 participants to compare PerspectEvolver and a baseline group-chat MAS.

The study evaluated how the different interaction designs affected research ideation outcomes and perceived cognitive

load (RQ1), feature use over a session (RQ2), and divergent and convergent thinking activities (RQ3).
5.2.1 Participants. We recruited 10 participants (Table 2), including engineering and science undergraduates, master’s

students, PhD students, and postdoctoral researchers with research experience ranging from 2 to 6 years (𝑀 = 3.60,

𝑆𝐷 = 1.43). Participants had diverse domain backgrounds and were recruited through university mailing lists and social

media platforms. The study was conducted remotely over Zoom12 and participants were compensated with $40. This

study was approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB).

5.2.2 Study Design and Procedure. We used a counterbalanced within-subjects design in which each participant

completed two hypothesis-generation tasks: one using PerspectEvolver and one using the baseline group-chat interface.

Participants submitted a scientific hypothesis on their research problem as the final output, consisting of four sections:

motivation, previous work, methodology, and expected results. Before the study, participants completed a pre-session

questionnaire covering demographics, research background, and frequency of LLM use. The questionnaire also collected

two research problems, one for each condition, which participants had prepared to meet two criteria: interest and

familiarity, in that the problem concerned a topic they were interested in and sufficiently familiar with; and comparable

difficulty, in that the two problems were similar in scope and complexity and demanded a similar level of effort and

domain knowledge.

Each session lasted approximately 120 minutes: a five-minute introduction covering the study goals and consent
procedures; two 50-minute tasks separated by a five-minute break; and a 10-minute semi-structured interview. Each

task lasted 50 minutes and comprised four phases. First, participants completed a five-minute walk-through of the

corresponding interface, in which the researcher demonstrated every feature using a sample research idea, and

participants practiced using the system. Second, participants completed a 10-minute solo phase in which they drafted

the four sections of a hypothesis while consulting papers on Google Scholar; this draft served as the point of comparison

for the final output. Third, 30 minutes interacting with the system. Fourth, a five-minute post-session survey covering

cognitive load, usability, and divergent and convergent thinking support, all on seven-point Likert scales. The session

concluded with a 10-minute semi-structured interview on participants’ experience with both systems, changes in their

scientific reasoning, the experience of configuring and evolving perspectives during hypothesis generation, divergent

and convergent thinking, and which features they found most helpful for scientific ideation (§A.2 in the appendix).

Throughout both tasks, participants were instructed to think aloud, verbalizing their thoughts, reactions, and decisions,

and we recorded these sessions for later analysis.

6.1.1 Improved Novelty of Written Hypotheses. Figure 6 shows how hypothesis quality changed from the initial draft

to the final version under each condition, as rated by an LLM judge on five dimensions (§5.3.1). We compare the

initial-to-final change in each arm, paired across participants.

Novelty improved substantially more under PerspectEvolver than under the baseline (M = +1.60 vs. −0.07, difference

+1.67, 𝑝 = .002∗∗ ), with all ten participants moving in the same direction. Plausibility (M = +0.44 vs. +0.15, 𝑝 = .641),

Significance (M = +0.15 vs. +0.02, 𝑝 = .297), and Testability (M = +0.37 vs. +0.11, 𝑝 = .373) leaned the same way without

Taken together, PerspectEvolver was associated with greater improvements in hypothesis novelty. Plausibility,
significance, and testability moved in the same direction, although none differed significantly from the baseline.

Evidence support moved in the opposite direction. This pattern may reflect a tension between novelty and existing

evidentiary support [34]: a hypothesis that prior work does not already entail may also be less strongly supported by

that prior work. These results suggest that supporting broader exploration may increase novelty while also increasing

the need to establish an evidentiary basis for newly developed directions.

6.1.2 Higher Usability and Lower Cognitive Load. PerspectEvolver was rated higher on usability than the baseline

(Figure 7; SUS: 𝑝 < .01∗∗ ; per-item results in Table 4 in the appendix). Participants described the session as straightforward

and quick to pick up, and said that the discussion and updated perspectives from each deliberation helped them grasp a

complex multi-agent exchange at a glance. As 𝑃8 explained, “Every step was intuitive, and I picked it up quickly.” 𝑃11

further noted, “There is a lot to think about when you are talking with several agents, but it was organized intuitively, and

having the whole thing in view on the canvas helped a great deal.”

PerspectEvolver also significantly reduced cognitive load (Figure 7; mental demand: 𝑝 < .01∗∗ ; effort: 𝑝 < .01∗∗ ;
frustration: 𝑝 = .109). Participants noted that the perspectives updated without their intervention, leaving them with

less to do between rounds. As 𝑃13 described, “Having the perspectives updated automatically was genuinely useful. It

cut down the burden a lot.” 𝑃7 added that the agents seemed to carry the earlier context with them, which made the

exchange easier to stay with, “It felt like talking together and keeping pace, rather than pulling something along on my

own.”

6.2

PerspectEvolver Achieves Design Goals: Configuring, Deliberating, and Evolving (RQ2)

In this section, we analyze system logs and think-aloud data to identify the designed affordances researchers drew on

and how they read the system and its agents.

6.2.1 Fewer Perspectives, Continually Evolved (DG1, DG3). Under PerspectEvolver, participants used a mean of 3.30
perspectives (SD 1.06) from 8.40 generated candidates drawn from a mean of 1,370 retrieved papers (SD 298). Building

a perspective from a cluster made the perspective’s stance easier to anticipate. As 𝑃8 explained, “Seeing the whole

thing on the cluster map and going by the representative paper for each cluster, the perspectives were easier to understand

than in the baseline.” 𝑃9 further noted, “Each paper came in six elements, so I could tell right away what the paper was

saying.” In contrast, the baseline produced more perspectives (M = 3.70, SD 1.16). In the baseline, participants configured

Perspectives directly from individual retrieved papers. This made a Perspective quick to construct when a suitable

paper was available, but the resulting Perspective could be narrower or less representative. As 𝑃10 described, “When it

Manuscript submitted to ACM

Anon.

was hard to find a paper that could represent a perspective, creating that perspective was hard and I wasn’t confident about

it. In the deliberation I didn’t really pay attention to that one.”

PerspectEvolver started from fewer perspectives than the baseline, but those perspectives went through a mean of

2.95 updates (SD 2.03). Each time a thread closes, the perspectives are updated against the working synthesis and the

researcher reviews the update. Over a session, then, a researcher obtained positions suited to how the discussion had

developed without configuring perspectives again. As 𝑃12 put it, “Every time the perspectives evolved, things moved along

with where the discussion was, so it felt like collaborating with a colleague. Like they already knew without me saying it.”

𝑃14 added, “Because the perspectives evolved, they picked up the context right away, so the load was definitely lower, and I

could use the AI more actively.”

Overall, participants used both construction and evolution: starting from fewer perspectives, a researcher could
carry a discussion through positions that fit the developing context, at a lower cost than rebuilding the panel.

6.2.2 Separate Threads, Visible Contributions, Recorded Revisions (DG2). Participants opened 42 threads in total; 27

were activated and 26 resolved, with a mean of 3.08 researcher turns per thread (SD 1.61, range = 1–7 turns). Threads

ran mostly in sequence: a thread was opened before the previous one closed on 5 occasions, and 4 of 10 participants ran

threads in parallel at least once. When a participant wanted one perspective’s opinion, they named that perspective

directly in the prompt. Interaction inside a thread took three broad shapes. Among the 26 resolved threads, 1) in

14 threads a participant drove the thread by asking perspectives directly: 𝑃10 put six consecutive questions to one

participant let the panel run on its own for several turns and then began questioning on the basis of that exchange: 𝑃11

took two continuations before stepping in (𝑃9 , 𝑃10 , 𝑃11 ); 3) In the last 5 threads a participant lifted a specific passage

from Working Synthesis and asked the authoring perspective to expand it: 𝑃2 sent a claim about closed-loop revision

failing under misleading verification feedback back to its authoring perspective for elaboration (𝑃 2 , 𝑃3 , 𝑃7 ). 10 of the 26

threads addressed a single perspective, and 16 addressed more than one.

After close interaction with the perspectives, each thread closed with a working synthesis, making visible what each
perspective contributed and driving revision of the hypothesis. The system proposed 91 revisions; participants accepted

64 (70.3%) as is, edited 25 (27.5%) before accepting, and rejected 2 (2.2%). Eighty-nine revisions reached the document

(M = 8.90, SD 4.15, range 4–17). Methodology and Expected Results received 26 revisions each, Previous Work 21, and

Framing 16, so the way a problem was approached and what it was expected to show changed more often than the

problem statement itself. 𝑃11 mentioned “It very helpful that perspective-guided deliberation made visible what each agent

had done and how it had contributed, and participants judged what to carry into the document with the source of each

contribution in view.” As 𝑃2 described the loop, “With the baseline it felt like I was passively steering something, but here it

felt like everyone was talking together with the same rhythm, and with more back and forth it came more naturally to

stretch my thinking.”

Deliberation and revision therefore worked as one loop rather than as separate stages. A participant directed the

discussion by addressing specific Perspectives, the discussion closed on a working synthesis showing which perspective

contributed what, and the decision to carry that into the document followed from the same view. Moving a discussion

into the document required no separate step, so a participant could run the loop several times within one session.

6.3

PerspectEvolver Facilitates Advancing Divergent and Convergent Thinking Activities (RQ3)

PerspectEvolver was rated as supporting both divergence and convergence (Figure 7; 3-item mean: 𝑝 < .01∗∗ ; per-item

results in Table 4 in the appendix). Participants explored new regions of the hypothesis space through PerspectEvolver

and developed what they found into a hypothesis; across that process, exploration and convergence felt like one

continuous movement rather than two separate efforts. 𝑃9 described expanding and then narrowing ideas as effortless

and reported that the support was most helpful during convergence, adding that the evolving perspectives were

something they could feel over the session. 𝑃 13 reported that the system helped with both divergence and convergence,

Discussion

In this section, we reflect on what designing and evaluating PerspectEvolver revealed, and draw three design implications

for multi-agent systems that support hypothesis generation. We then discuss the limitations of the present work and

directions for future research.

7.1

Beyond Configuration: Evolving Perspectives in Human–AI Sensemaking

Sensemaking in human–AI research ideation involves both constructing multiple perspectives on a problem and evolving

those perspectives as new evidence and interpretations reshape the research direction. As the research direction

develops, researchers must assess how each perspective interprets the problem and whether it remains relevant to the
research being pursued.

Prior multi-agent ideation systems primarily support configuring perspectives, while subsequent changes are

manual or implicit [29, 30, 40]. PerspectEvolver makes perspective evolution inspectable by externalizing how each

Perspective contributes to the developing research direction and how its interpretation shifts over time. Participants

using PerspectEvolver achieved greater improvements in hypothesis novelty, rated the system higher in usability, and

reported lower cognitive load. Unresolved disagreements and questions can redirect research by identifying aspects of

the problem that require further examination. PerspectEvolver preserves these issues as follow-up questions, allowing

outcomes from earlier deliberation to shape what researchers investigate next.
Beyond scientific research, this approach may apply to domains in which work develops through deliberation among

multiple perspectives. Such settings require that (1) perspectives can be represented through interpretable elements,

(2) deliberation outcomes can be traced to the perspectives that shaped them, and (3) those outcomes can revise the

perspectives used as the work develops. For example, in legal counsel, (1) perspectives can be represented through issue,

governing doctrine, scope, and anticipated counterargument [13]; (2) deliberation can establish a provisional position

on the governing doctrine while preserving which perspectives and authorities shaped that position and leaving issues

such as damage calculation unresolved; and (3) subsequent argument or evidence can revise those perspectives as

unresolved issues are examined further.

7.2

Design Implication 1: Externalize Grounding, Preserve Researcher Judgment

Under PerspectEvolver, a larger share of participants’ coded activities involved exploring, synthesizing, and selecting

ideas, while a smaller share involved comparison, prior knowledge, evaluation, and anchoring. This shift accompanied

greater improvements in hypothesis novelty, suggesting that reducing the effort required to reconstruct and ground

prior reasoning can leave more attention for developing the research direction.

The formative study shows what this grounding required (§3.3.3). Participants had to reconstruct what prior

discussions had established, trace ideas to the perspectives that introduced them, and determine how discussion

outcomes should affect the hypothesis and the perspectives. These are forms of information the system can externalize

through persistent threads, contribution provenance, and linked revisions, reducing the need for researchers to recover

them from conversation history [15, 19].

Ideation systems have largely focused on generating and narrowing candidate ideas. This addresses only part of

the grounding required when the perspectives used to interpret those ideas also develop. When a research direction

changes while those perspectives remain fixed, researchers must reconcile the developing direction with interpretations

that no longer reflect it [25]. Maintaining this correspondence becomes recurring grounding work as ideation continues.

Grounding of this kind can be absorbed because such grounding is a matter of record rather than judgment: what a

thread settled, where a claim originated, and which insights bear on which perspective are facts the system already

holds and can keep in view. What the system should not absorb is the decision that follows—which candidate to retain,

merge, or set aside—since no body of prior work determines what is worth pursuing in a researcher’s own inquiry [18].

A system that supports divergence and convergence should therefore be designed to carry the grounding that recovers

what has already been produced, while leaving the researcher to advance the hypothesis.

The principle extends from the individual perspective to the ensemble. When a new thread takes up a different

aspect of the research problem, the ensemble assembled for earlier threads becomes less relevant to what is now under

discussion, and that mismatch is itself an occasion for grounding: the same literature that grounds a perspective’s

revision can be searched for the perspectives the new thread calls for. A system that supports this loop—retrieving

relevant perspectives, recomposing the ensemble for the thread that follows—would allow researchers to sustain

divergent and convergent thinking with the perspectives each stage of the inquiry requires.

7.3

Design Implication 2: Friction Needs an Outlet

Prior work holds that friction in ideation systems is productive: exposing researchers to opposing views leads them to

scrutinize what an agent proposes rather than accept it outright [7, 27, 30]. In design ideation, agents that challenge

the dominant proposal reduce design fixation [27]; in group decision-making, devil’s-advocate agents voice minority

positions against conformity pressure [7]. However, friction is not always beneficial. Cognitive forcing interventions

improve the accuracy of judgment, but lower user satisfaction and trust, and counterargument agents loosen the

discussion at the cost of higher cognitive load [26, 50]. More fundamentally, friction that never resolves closes off new

ideation rather than opening it, producing idea fixation or premature convergence [28].

Our design gives unresolved friction a different meaning. Where the perspectives converge, the agreement enters

the hypothesis; where they do not, the disagreement becomes an open question that configures the perspectives for the

next round. Treating the unresolved as material for the next round makes it not a stalled state but a signal that the topic

requires a new ensemble of perspectives. A system that introduces counterarguments to support diversity in ideation

must therefore not only consider their strength and frequency, but also provide an outlet for the friction they produce.

Without an outlet, even a well-placed counterargument remains a burden the researcher must carry; with one, the same

counterargument becomes material for the next step.

However, opening the next round automatically from what remains unresolved carries its own cost. If a researcher

does not follow why the loop reopened where it did, the loop that never closes becomes a trap of a different kind from

fixation [36, 48], and our system offers no account of when the space has been explored enough. Two forms of support

are therefore required. The first is an agent-generated account of the provenance of each perspective revision and

each suggested thread. The second is a macro-level representation of the investigation’s trajectory, through which
researchers can assess whether the space has been explored sufficiently.

7.4

Design Implication 3: Extending Ensembles by Evolution Rather Than Addition

Widening the hypothesis space an ensemble of perspectives covers encourages new ideas. If perspectives can only

be revised manually as an idea develops, the only way to extend the ensemble to fit the context is to add agents, and

cognitive load rises accordingly [38]. Our work instead kept the number of perspectives small, supported sensemaking

of how perspectives evolve, and produced hypotheses rated more novel than in the baseline.

An evolved Perspective accumulates changes across rounds while remaining a single inspectable unit. Because

that unit is made explicit, each element showing what the perspective now holds, the researcher works with the unit

rather than with the individual changes inside it, a form of chunking that suggests load need not scale with what each

perspective has accumulated [9]. This points to a further dimension along which an ensemble can be extended: not

only the number of perspectives present, but the capacity of those perspectives to develop through deliberation.

Room for improvement remains in how far sensemaking of perspective evolution reduces load. As deliberation

proceeds and evolved perspectives converge, contrast weakens, and admitting a new perspective into the ensemble

can raise the load again. As 𝑃10 mentioned, When I bring a new agent into the discussion, it is still hard to decide what

belongs in the ensemble. A recommendation that reflects the context of my deliberation would free more energy for creative

decisions.” Supporting sensemaking of perspective evolution may therefore defer the point at which load arises rather

than remove the load itself: while perspective evolution sustains diversity, the ensemble needs no recomposition, and

the cost of selection returns only once evolution alone no longer produces contrast. For cognitive load, this points to

recommending new perspectives from the context of the deliberation and the researcher’s intent, rather than leaving
that selection to the researcher alone.

7.5

Limitations and Future Work

First, the duration of the user study (30 minutes) and the size of the participant pool (N = 10) may limit the generalizability

of our findings. Participants discovered several high-quality hypotheses within the session, but in research practice a

promising hypothesis takes shape over weeks and several meetings. The ten participants may also not fully represent

the space of research ideation domains. For future work, we plan to collaborate with more researchers and to conduct a

long-term study of at least one week to enhance the generalizability of PerspectEvolver.

Second, using an LLM as a judge to evaluate submitted hypotheses may introduce bias. Even though the judge’s

ratings agreed with expert ratings (Krippendorff’s 𝛼 = 0.75), that agreement might not guarantee the ratings reflect what

researchers in the field consider promising. Future work should complement automated evaluation with larger-scale

expert assessment.

PerspectEvolver: Evolving Perspectives through Multi-Agent Deliberation for Research Ideation

Conclusion

Multi-agent systems for ideation have focused on assigning diverse perspectives to agents, yet have largely left how a

researcher’s perspectives evolve through deliberation to the user. We presented PerspectEvolver, an interactive MAS

that supports the sensemaking required to configure and evolve a perspective ensemble: each perspective is built from

a cluster of literature and represented as six inspectable elements, deliberation runs in topic threads that condense into

a statement of what settled and what remains open, and that statement revises the hypothesis and the perspectives

while what no thread settled opens the round that follows. In a within-subjects study with 10 science and engineering

researchers, PerspectEvolver produced greater improvements in hypothesis novelty and was rated higher in usability and

support for divergent and convergent thinking, while participants reported lower cognitive load. Advancing utterances

also accounted for a larger share of the session under PerspectEvolver, suggesting that effort otherwise spent recovering

and organizing what a discussion had produced was available instead for moving the inquiry forward. We hope this

work encourages the design of multi-agent systems in which perspectives evolve explicitly as deliberation unfolds.

Interview Questions

• Can you walk me through your overall experience developing your hypothesis in each session?

A.3

Five dimensions for evaluating hypotheses

We evaluated the quality of submitted hypotheses on the five dimensions with the LLM judge (GPT-5.6 Luna) below,
• Novelty: the degree to which the hypothesis is not already entailed by prior work Si et al. [45].
• Plausibility: whether the hypothesis is consistent with established scientific principles and theories [12].

• Significance: the potential importance of the hypothesis if validated Yang et al. [49].

• Testability: whether the hypothesis can be practically tested with available techniques, equipment, and re-

sources [12].
• Evidence Support: the extent to which the hypothesis is grounded in, or verifiable against, existing evidence Ke
et al. [21].

Technical Details of PerspectEvolver

PerspectEvolver comprises Perspective Agents, the Deliberation Space, and the Deliberation Protocol.

A.4.1 Perspective Agents. A Perspective Agent is a research collaborator defined by persistent Perspective state and

shared generation modules.

• Role. Develops ideas, addresses research questions, and distinguishes evidence from interpretation.

• Perspective. A scientific focus, Framing, Position, and PerspectiveFragments for Scope, Explanation, Approach,

and Significance.

• Knowledge. Source-linked papers, passages, and Literature Evidence.

• Working Context. The current Perspective, Investigation Brief, thread context, and researcher request.

• Interaction History. Prior Contributions, retrieved evidence, syntheses, and revisions.

• Interaction Protocol. Rules for participation and response within the Perspective Ensemble.

• Model and Tools. Language-model, retrieval, and reranking components.

A.4.3 Deliberation Protocol. The Deliberation Protocol governs turns, response targets, evidence requests, and
synthesis.

• DeliberationPolicy. Speaker and target selection and stopping conditions.

• DiscussionRound. A researcher-requested sequence of agent turns.

• TurnAction. One action with its Perspective, target, instruction, and optional MoveType.

• Contribution. An authored response with scientific statements, target, type, quotation, and evidence references.

• EvidenceRequest. An unresolved empirical question and literature query.

Implementation. Discussion rounds select speakers and targets until the requested turns complete or the exchange stops.

An EvidenceRequest searches Perspective literature, the investigation corpus, and external literature when configured,

then extracts source-linked Literature Evidence. A WorkingSynthesis uses Contributions, cited Literature Evidence,

and evidence-request outcomes. The system extracts provenance-linked DiscussionInputs that distinguish empirical

findings from scientific considerations. Reflection compares these inputs with each Perspective and returns no_change,

update_position, or update_framing_and_position; proposed changes retain their motivating inputs and previous

content. Investigation Brief revisions and follow-up questions are generated separately from the Working Synthesis,

with LangGraph pausing for researcher review before document changes are applied.
