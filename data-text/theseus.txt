THESEUS: Iterative Hypothesis Validation with Evolving Branch Structures,
Traceable Rationale, and Researcher-Steered Replanning
ANONYMOUS AUTHOR(S)
Hypothesis validation requires decomposing broad claims, designing experiments, and revising plans as evidence accumulates. Existing

AI tools assist individual stages or automate discovery end-to-end with limited human involvement, but provide limited support

for maintaining evolving relationships among hypotheses, experiments, results, and rationale. We present THESEUS, a human–AI

system that organizes this validation state as an evolving branch structure with literature-grounded rationales, supporting branch-

specific result interpretation and experiment- and hypothesis-level replanning while researchers decide which proposed revisions to

adopt. In a lab study (𝑁 = 16), participants rated THESEUS higher than a strong linear AI baseline on plan organization, rationale
traceability, and control, while reporting lower workload. In a one-week comparison (𝑁 = 10), explicit result–hypothesis links helped
researchers compare next steps and revise affected branches selectively. These findings highlight branch-structured validation state
and researcher-steered replanning as design directions for scientific AI.

Introduction

Recent AI systems increasingly support scientists in generating research ideas and hypotheses [1, 45, 46, 61], but

scientific work does not end once a hypothesis is proposed. A broad hypothesis is rarely resolved by a single experiment;

instead, evidence accumulates across multiple tests, often leaving some claims supported, others uncertain, and new

explanations to consider. Scientific reasoning research characterizes this process as an iterative coordination between

hypotheses and experiments [38], while the Hierarchy of Hypotheses approach emphasizes decomposing broad claims

into testable branches and connecting evidence at the appropriate level [29]. We focus on this process of hypothesis

validation, in which hypotheses and experimental plans evolve as evidence accumulates.

Effective hypothesis validation therefore requires scientists to coordinate several forms of reasoning over time. First,
they must maintain an evolving branch structure that makes explicit how broad hypotheses, sub-hypotheses, and

experiments relate to one another. Second, they must keep the rationale and evidence supporting these hypotheses and

experiments traceable, so that researchers can understand why a branch was introduced, how an experiment tests it,

and what evidence bears on it. Third, they must connect result interpretation to subsequent replanning: an unexpected

or inconclusive result may motivate repeating or modifying an experiment, testing another mechanism, revising a

sub-hypothesis, or pursuing an alternative branch. Together, these demands make hypothesis validation an ongoing

coordination problem rather than a sequence of isolated experiments and analyses.

evidence and deciding how it should change an investigation require scientific judgment, we focus on human–AI support
in which AI can propose interpretations and next steps while consequential revisions remain subject to researcher
review and adoption.
In this work, we investigate how scientists manage hypothesis validation as evidence accumulates and how AI can
support this process. We first conducted an artifact-centered formative interview study [13] with ten researchers. Using
participants’ own planning documents, protocols, notes, slides, analysis files, and experimental results, we identified

three recurring breakdowns: Challenge 1, mental-model mismatch between branching hypothesis reasoning and linear,

fragmented artifacts required researchers to reconstruct relationships across their investigations; Challenge 2, the

rationale and evidence behind hypotheses and experiments were often implicit and difficult to trace; and Challenge 3,
noisy or ambiguous results were difficult to translate into situated next steps. Guided by these findings, we designed

THESEUS, a human–AI system for iterative hypothesis validation (Figure 1). THESEUS supports this process through

three connected capabilities corresponding to our design goals. First, to address Challenge 1, THESEUS externalizes an

evolving branch structure connecting broad hypotheses, testable sub-hypotheses, experiments, and accumulated results
(DG1). Second, to address Challenge 2, it connects hypotheses and experiments with supporting literature, rationale,

and evidence so that these relationships remain explicit and inspectable as the investigation evolves (DG2). Third, to

address Challenge 3, it interprets new results in the context of the corresponding branch and supports experiment- and

hypothesis-level replanning by presenting possible revisions for researcher review (DG3).
To evaluate THESEUS, we conducted a two-phase user study comparing it with a strong linear AI baseline that

provided the same core AI functions. Both conditions used the same underlying AI model and backend workflows and

provided the same categories of AI-generated scientific content, while the baseline delivered this support through an

editable linear document and function-specific conversations. In Phase I, a lab study with 16 scientists, participants
selected two hypotheses from their own research, matched them with the study team for comparable scope and difficulty,

and used one in each condition to construct and revise a hypothesis-validation plan. In Phase II, 10 participants continued

working with the same matched hypotheses as they conducted experiments, brought experimental results back into

the systems, interpreted the evidence, and decided how to proceed. Participants rated THESEUS significantly higher
in structure and organization (𝑝 H < .001), rationale and traceability (𝑝 H < .001), steerability and control (𝑝 H < .001),

and result interpretation and next-step support (𝑝 H = .010), while also reporting lower workload. For blinded expert

assessment, we converted the Phase I outputs from both conditions into standardized linear validation-plan documents.

Experts rated THESEUS plans higher on most assessed dimensions, with the largest difference in evidence–hypothesis
traceability, although the improvement in feasibility and actionability did not remain significant after correction.

Qualitative findings showed that evolving branch structure and explicit result–hypothesis links reduced the need to

reconstruct prior context, helped participants compare alternative next steps, and supported local revision without

Related Work

2.1

Prior work characterizes hypothesis validation as a non-linear coordination between hypotheses, experiments, and

accumulating evidence [38]. Because an experiment typically bears most directly on a particular sub-hypothesis, a local

result may weaken one branch without invalidating its parent hypothesis or alternatives. Laboratory studies show

that unexpected results can expose incorrect assumptions and motivate competing explanations [15, 16]. Scientists

must also coordinate conceptual hypotheses with computational models, experimental measurements, operational

variables, and executable analyses across repeated cycles [17, 34]. A longitudinal study of biomedical research further

demonstrates that hypothesis work extends across data exploration, literature synthesis, external representations, and

investigation histories [43].

Scientific communities have developed several approaches to manage this complexity. The hierarchy-of-hypotheses

approach organizes broad hypotheses and sub-hypotheses so evidence can be associated with appropriate branches [29],

while natural-language formalization makes specification, decomposition, and operationalization relationships ex-

plicit [30]. Methods of multiple working hypotheses preserve competing explanations rather than prematurely selecting

one account [9, 60]. Computational tools support literature-based discovery, hypothesis generation, idea refinement,

and analysis planning [14, 45, 46, 50, 61]. More directly, Reasoning Maps externalize relationships among hypotheses,

experiments, results, and conclusions [58]; confirmatory visualization systems compare conceptual expectations with

observed data [7, 8]; and a Grammar of Hypotheses formalizes relationships among data, analysis questions, and visual-

ization capabilities [53]. Visual belief elicitation can further improve inferential accuracy and reduce false discovery [40],

while HypoChainer supports hypothesis exploration and validation prioritization through LLMs, knowledge graphs,

and visualization [32].

These approaches also connect to a broader HCI tradition of externalizing reasoning, alternatives, and histories of
exploratory work. Design-rationale systems such as gIBIS showed how decisions, arguments, and their justifications

can be represented explicitly [11]. Systems for analytic provenance and exploratory programming similarly preserve

intermediate states, alternatives, and revision histories so that users can revisit how an analysis developed. VisTrails

2.2

AI Systems for Scientific Research

AI systems increasingly support scientific workflows involving literature synthesis, hypothesis generation, experiment
design, execution, and result analysis [26, 55]. Coscientist combines language-model planning with literature search,
code execution, and laboratory tools for chemical experimentation [5]. The AI Scientist and Agent Laboratory support
workflows spanning idea generation, experimentation, analysis, and report writing [41, 48]. Google’s AI Co-Scientist
generates, debates, ranks, and refines biomedical hypotheses [25], while Robin connects literature-based hypotheses
with experiment planning, data analysis, and subsequent refinement [22]. LabOS extends AI support into physical
laboratories through multimodal perception, extended-reality interfaces, and real-time assistance during biomedical

experiments [10]. Complementing these broad workflow systems, other work focuses more directly on hypothesis

testing and iterative experimentation. POPPER translates hypotheses into executable falsification tests [31], while

LATCH and VERITAS conduct analyses over clinical and multimodal medical data [23, 52]. AutoScientists organizes
research teams around competing hypotheses, conducts experiments in parallel, and records successful results and

failed directions in a shared experimental state [20]. Related systems maintain hypothesis lineage, evidence, belief

changes, experimental plans, and repair histories throughout scientific workflows [54, 56]. These systems demonstrate

the value of maintaining state across iterative scientific workflows, but primarily use that state to coordinate, execute,
or audit broader AI-driven research processes. They provide less support for organizing scientist-facing validation

around explicit sub-hypothesis branches in which researchers can inspect why a particular experiment bears on a claim,

situate a returned result within that branch, and determine how that local evidence should affect subsequent actions.

Successfully executing a research workflow also does not imply that model-generated scientific reasoning is reliable.
DiscoveryBench and Scientific Discovery Evaluation identify difficulties with multistep discovery, experiment design, and

result interpretation [42, 51], while EXP-Bench reports only a 0.5% success rate for complete executable experiments [39].

Other evaluations find that AI systems may fail to revise their reasoning appropriately after receiving experimental

feedback [28, 47]. These findings motivate interaction designs in which model-generated interpretations and revisions
remain inspectable and contestable rather than being treated as authoritative scientific decisions. In this work, we

therefore ask how AI can support scientist-led validation while keeping evidence linked to the specific sub-hypothesis it

bears on, situating interpretations within the surrounding branch, and leaving consequential changes to the validation

structure under researcher control.

Formative Interview Study and Prototype Co-Design

We conducted an artifact-centered formative interview study to identify recurring challenges in scientists’ iterative

hypothesis-validation practices. We then conducted a prototype co-design study with a group of researchers to translate

these challenges into concrete interaction requirements and design goals. All participant-facing studies reported in

this paper were reviewed and approved by our institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB). Participants provided
informed consent before participation, including consent for audio recording and the use of anonymized quotations.

3.1

3.1.1 Participants. We recruited ten researchers, five Ph.D. students and five research scientists, through personal

Formative Study

networks and targeted email outreach. Participants worked in biology, chemistry, materials science, or related fields

and had between three and nine years of research experience. To be eligible, participants needed to be actively

engaged in empirical research and to have recently worked on a project involving repeated hypothesis development,

experimentation, result interpretation, and revision. Appendix A reports participants’ anonymized demographic and
research backgrounds. Each participant received a $40 gift card.

3.1.2 Procedure. We conducted 45–60-minute semi-structured interviews online or in person, following an artifact-

centered interview approach [13]. Each participant selected a recent or ongoing project centered on a scientific

hypothesis and involving multiple rounds of empirical investigation. We examined how participants organized and

revisited their validation work across artifacts and tools, with particular attention to how they maintained relationships

among different parts of the investigation and responded to new experimental evidence. These discussions were

grounded in participants’ own artifacts, including laboratory notes, presentation slides, manuscripts, and AI chat

histories. We probed which hypothesis or assumption an experiment examined, what motivated the experiment, what

alternative explanations were considered, and how particular results led to subsequent actions.

With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed. We analyzed transcripts and notes

using open coding followed by thematic analysis. Initial codes captured concrete practices and difficulties, including

keeping hypotheses and experiments across separate artifacts, recalling which experiment tested which hypothesis,

recovering why an experiment had been conducted, leaving rationale in conversation or memory, re-explaining project

context to AI tools, and deciding what to do after reviewing a result. We grouped related codes into three recurring

challenges in participants’ hypothesis-validation workflows.

3.1.3

Findings.

Challenge 1: Mental-model mismatch between branching reasoning and linear, fragmented artifacts. Participants

consistently described hypothesis validation as a branching process, yet the artifacts they used to represent this work

were often linear or fragmented. This created a mismatch between how participants mentally organized relationships

among hypotheses, experiments, and accumulating evidence and how those relationships were externally represented.

A broad hypothesis could be decomposed into several sub-hypotheses, each examined through different experiments,

controls, or conditions; as results accumulated, branches could be continued, modified, suspended, or replaced. However,

all ten participants reported that their existing artifacts captured only parts of this evolving structure. Laboratory

notebooks, protocols, spreadsheets, analysis files, presentation slides, papers, and AI conversations typically recorded

individual hypotheses, plans, or results rather than the relationships among them. P3, for example, explained that links

among hypotheses, data, interpretations, and decisions were often worked out through discussions with collaborators

rather than "fully represented in one structured artifact." P4 similarly relied on Word documents, papers, AI conversations,
and personal memory, but had no explicit structure showing how assumptions changed over time or how one result led
to a new direction.
Because this branching mental model was not persistently externalized, participants had to reconstruct the branch
structure from scattered artifacts and memory when revisiting a project, preparing for meetings, or explaining their work
to collaborators. Nine participants explicitly described this recovery as difficult or incomplete. P6 described keeping track

of the structure "using my mind," which created "much cognitive load," and wanted to see a "whole tree of hypotheses"

that could be examined one branch or level at a time. The challenge was therefore not that hypotheses, experiments, or

results were undocumented, but that their branching relationships did not persist in an external representation aligned
with how participants reasoned about the investigation. Researchers consequently had to repeatedly reconstruct which
experiments tested which sub-hypotheses and how intermediate evidence had changed the investigation.

Challenge 2: Literature-grounded rationale remained implicit and difficult to trace. Beyond reconstructing what was

connected, participants struggled to recover why a particular sub-hypothesis, experiment, interpretation, or next step

had been considered reasonable. All ten participants described at least some rationale as implicit or incompletely

documented. Their reasoning drew on prior literature, domain knowledge, experimental experience, mechanistic

assumptions, and conversations with collaborators, but these sources were rarely linked explicitly to the hypothesis-

validation relationships they supported. As a result, participants’ artifacts more reliably preserved what they had done

than why they had done it. P7 described the relationship between a result and the subsequent decision as "clear in the

mind, but not written down." P8 similarly identified the "rationale and linkage" between evidence of molecular binding

and a subsequent claim about biological function as difficult to capture, while P10 noted that "the link and rationale is

not explicitly shown."

This missing epistemic traceability became especially consequential after multiple iterations. Participants could often
recover protocols, data, or final conclusions but not the full reasoning behind why one modification or explanation

had been selected over another. When rationale remained in memory or conversation, researchers had to reconstruct

the basis for earlier decisions and could not always recover it completely. The problem was therefore not a lack of

scientific reasons, but the absence of a persistent external record linking literature and assumptions to sub-hypotheses,

experiments, results, interpretations, and decisions. This made rationale difficult to inspect, communicate, and revise as
the investigation evolved.
Challenge 3: Actionable insight gap between interpretation and next-step decisions. Across all ten cases, participants

described situations in which interpreting a result did not uniquely determine what to do next. An unexpected, noisy,

or weak result could support several explanations and lead researchers to repeat an experiment, add a control, modify a

protocol, test another sub-hypothesis, or revise the broader hypothesis. P1 explained that after annealing, "it is not just
one variable changing," because the material, metal stack, anchor structure, and stress state could all change together. P2

similarly noted that a failed output could arise from the assay, robotic operation, sample, readout, or analysis, and that

"a bad final result does not automatically tell me which part failed." In both cases, the result indicated that something

had changed or failed without identifying which explanation should be investigated first.
Participants therefore needed to compare several possible actions. P4, for example, considered changing the electrode

material, measurement arrangement, substrate, wire connection, or machine settings, repeating the measurement,

testing an artificial tissue model, or reconsidering the sub-hypothesis itself. These options were evaluated based on

factors such as feasibility, signal quality, relevance to the main hypothesis, and their ability to distinguish remaining

3.2

Prototype Co-Design Study

The formative interviews identified three recurring challenges, but they did not specify how a system should address

these challenges in practice. We therefore developed an early branch-based prototype and used it as a co-design probe

with six additional researchers. Rather than evaluating a finished design, this study examined how participants wanted

to construct, inspect, and revise an evolving hypothesis-validation structure, and what forms of AI assistance, editing,
and control they considered appropriate.

3.2.1 Participants and Procedure. The prototype represented hypotheses, sub-hypotheses, experiments, results, inter-

pretations, and next-step decisions in a connected graph and included preliminary AI-generated rationales, experiment

plans, expected outcomes, result interpretations, and suggested next steps. We recruited six researchers with experi-

ence in empirical biological research, including three Ph.D. students and three research scientists. Each completed an

approximately 30-minute semi-structured walkthrough using a hypothesis from a current project, previous project, or

published paper.

During the walkthrough, participants inspected and critiqued the generated graph, including whether it matched
their understanding of the investigation, what they would change before using a proposed experiment, whether the

evidence and rationale were sufficient to evaluate a claim, how realistic results should be recorded, and what operations

they expected at the end of a branch. In the concluding interview, they distinguished information they wanted AI to

propose from decisions they wanted to make themselves and suggested missing interactions. We consolidated recurring

feedback into four concrete interaction outcomes.
3.2.2

Co-Design Outcomes.

Co-Design Outcome 1: Editable experiment plans across multiple experimental contexts. Participants treated AIgenerated experiment plans as useful starting points but emphasized that protocols must remain editable before

and after execution. They wanted to modify parameters such as concentration, volume, temperature, duration, controls,

and measurement procedures without regenerating the entire plan. They also noted that the same experimental logic

might be applied across different cell lines, organisms, treatment groups, materials, or sample contexts. This led us

to support editable protocol fields and context-specific experiment variants connected to the same sub-hypothesis,
preserving a shared experimental purpose without creating disconnected copies.
Co-Design Outcome 2: Explicit expected outcomes and inspectable model confidence. Participants wanted the system to

specify in advance which outcomes would support, weaken, or leave a sub-hypothesis unresolved. Making these expec-

tations explicit could help researchers assess whether an experiment can discriminate among competing explanations

and reduce post-hoc reinterpretation of results. Participants also wanted an explicit indication of how confident the

AI was in proposed hypothesis decompositions and experiment–hypothesis relationships, so that lower-confidence

suggestions could receive greater scrutiny. They emphasized that such confidence cues should support inspection rather

than replace scientific judgment.

Co-Design Outcome 3: Replanning operations at both the experiment and hypothesis levels. Participants distinguished
changes to an experiment from changes to the hypothesis structure. An inconclusive result might call for another

replicate, an additional control, or a redesigned protocol, whereas evidence against an explanation might motivate

an alternative sub-hypothesis or broader revision. They wanted explicit end-of-branch operations for repeating or

modifying an experiment, creating another experiment for the same claim, introducing an alternative sub-hypothesis,
and merging related branches. Participants wanted these operations to preserve prior results and decision rationales

and, ideally, make earlier versions recoverable so that changing direction would not erase the reasoning that produced

the current state.

Co-Design Outcome 4: Structured result entry that accommodates experimental complexity. Participants found a

single free-text result field insufficient for experiments involving multiple samples, conditions, measurements, files, or

replicates, while also noting that a rigid schema would not fit diverse procedures. Their feedback led to result entry

supporting multiple observations and attachments, along with adaptable tables containing fields such as condition,

sample or cell type, replicate, measurement, and notes. This keeps results associated with the relevant experimental

context while leaving control over the required fields to researchers.

Collectively, these outcomes shifted the prototype from an AI-generated visualization into a co-experimentation
system in which scientists could edit plans, inspect evidence, record heterogeneous results, compare possible actions,
and determine when the validation structure should change.

3.3

Design Goals

Synthesizing the formative interview findings with the prototype co-design outcomes, we derived three higher-level
design goals that capture the core forms of support THESEUS should provide for iterative hypothesis validation.

DG1: Externalize hypothesis validation as an evolving branch structure (Challenge 1). Researchers described hypothesis

validation as branching, yet the linear and fragmented artifacts they used did not preserve these relationships, creating

a mismatch between how they organized the investigation and how it was externally represented. We thus aim to

provide an editable branch-based representation that preserves these relationships and helps researchers follow how

different validation branches evolve over time.

DG2: Externalize literature-grounded rationale behind hypothesis-validation links (Challenge 2). Researchers often

relied on literature, domain knowledge, and experimental assumptions to justify how hypotheses were decomposed and

why particular experiments were selected, but these rationales frequently remained implicit or scattered across artifacts.

We thus aim to make the supporting literature, evidence, assumptions, and reasoning behind each hypothesis-validation

link explicit and inspectable.

DG3: Support context-aware result interpretation and experiment- and hypothesis-level replanning. (Challenge 3).

Researchers found it difficult to choose the next action when noisy or ambiguous results were compatible with multiple

explanations. We thus aim to help researchers interpret results within the context of the surrounding validation branch,

compare experiment-level and hypothesis-level next steps, and decide which proposed changes are incorporated into

the evolving plan.

THESEUS: Traceable Hypothesis-validation system with Evolving Structure, sourced Evidence, and

User-Steered replanning

Guided by our design goals, we present THESEUS (Fig. 2), a graph-based human–AI system for iterative hypothesis

validation. THESEUS uses GPT-5.4-mini for its LLM agents and LangGraph to orchestrate their workflow. THESEUS

represents the evolving validation plan as a directed graph connecting a root hypothesis and its testable sub-hypotheses
with experiments (DG1). Result and follow-up nodes show how new evidence affects each branch. Supporting literature

and rationale remain accessible for inspection, together with the assumptions and criteria that guide result interpretation

(DG2). Hypothesis-edge scores indicate confidence in the proposed parent–child decomposition, while experiment-edge

scores indicate confidence that an experiment can test its linked hypotheses. When experimental results arrive, THESEUS
interprets them within the context of the corresponding experiment and hypothesis branch and presents concrete,

reviewable next steps (DG3). Throughout this process, the AI proposes scientific content, while the researcher reviews

and edits proposals and decides which proposed changes are committed to the evolving validation state. Figures 2–3

illustrate the workflow from hypothesis decomposition and experiment planning to result interpretation and replanning.

retrieves relevant literature and method evidence, then proposes a directed graph of sub-hypotheses (Appendix B.2).

literature (Fig. 2–C). Blocked candidates from the same run remain available in this panel. The researcher can manually

enter a new sub-hypothesis under the root or an approved non-leaf hypothesis. Each proposal undergoes the same

critique process and is added to the map if approved. For an approved leaf, she can request further decomposition,
extending the selected branch by one level with optional guidance (Fig. 2–E).

4.2

Planning Experiments and Recording Observations

Every sub-hypothesis is formulated as a testable statement. In the initial map, THESEUS creates an experiment family
for each leaf hypothesis at the configured decomposition depth (Fig. 2–D). It also creates group-scoped families for

compatible leaves that can be tested together. Each family groups one base experiment with zero or more variants in a

single node, keeping the map compact. Variant labels identify the factor and level that distinguish each variant from the

base, such as a different dose or model. For an approved leaf hypothesis, the researcher can also submit an experiment

plan in her own words, together with a confidence rating of how well it tests the hypothesis. THESEUS reviews the

plan and generates a corresponding experiment package and protocol. For each experiment, a protocol-writing LLM

combines the proposed design, mapped hypotheses, method information, and laboratory context with available public

protocol references to generate a step-by-step protocol (Appendix B.3). A separate LLM reviewer checks the draft for

alignment with the hypotheses, consistency of the intervention, controls, and readouts, and measurement feasibility.

The researcher selects a base or variant experiment to open its full-width protocol view (Fig. 3–F, G). Appendix B.1

presents the layout of this interface and explains its individual sections. The accompanying design explains the

experiment’s purpose and specifies what will be varied and measured within its experimental system. Controls and

assumptions are documented alongside the expected patterns so that the researcher can evaluate the proposed test

before conducting it. She compares family members and reviews the proposed controls, readouts, and step-by-step

protocol to assess whether they fit her laboratory’s practice, then edits the selected protocol as needed. Saving applies

the edits to the selected experiment’s protocol while leaving other family members unchanged.

After conducting the experiment, the researcher records the observed values and outcomes in the selected member’s

CSV template and uploads the completed file (Fig. 3–H). THESEUS verifies that the submission matches the experiment

package and covers every hypothesis mapped to it. Results remain associated with that specific member, so an upload

for the base experiment does not become evidence for a sibling variant. Repeated rows are preserved as replicates, and

the source file is retained with the saved analysis. Each analysis remains linked to the experiment and hypotheses it

evaluated. Subsequent edits to the selected protocol invalidate its associated analysis, while analyses for unchanged

experiments remain current.

4.3

Interpreting Evidence and Replanning

For each experiment–hypothesis pair, THESEUS interprets the submitted observations in relation to the hypothesis and

its canonical protocol context (Fig. 3–H). THESEUS obtains three assessments from separate invocations of the same

model using distinct prompts: an Evidence Analyst examines observed patterns, an Experimental Validity Reviewer

examines the adequacy of the experimental evidence, and a Hypothesis Inference Reviewer examines whether the

observations support the scope of the claim (Appendix F.4.1). Each invocation receives the same evidence and returns a

vote on the whole mapped hypothesis with a concise rationale. Deterministic majority voting aggregates these votes

into a model-generated interpretation label: approved or not approved by the submitted result. The label summarizes

whether the observed results support the hypothesis under the tested conditions. This decision remains specific to the

submitted evidence and serves as a review aid for the researcher.

To determine whether replanning is needed, THESEUS considers all experiments connected to the hypothesis (Fig. 3–

I). Follow-up options remain unavailable until every connected experiment has a current analysis. If all current analyses

receive an approved-by-result label, THESEUS indicates that no corrective follow-up is currently suggested by the model.

Otherwise, the system presents two directions: replace the hypothesis or run further experiments. The researcher selects

which direction to explore before THESEUS generates three preview candidates. These candidates are proposals: the

validation state changes only when the researcher submits a candidate for adoption. Submitted replacement hypotheses

4.4

Discussing and Revisiting the system

At any point, the researcher can select a hypothesis or experiment and open a discussion drawer scoped to that node

and its nearby branch. The discussion agent explains the current rationale or drafts structured field edits, including

changes to protocol steps. Drafts remain pending until the researcher applies or discards them. A draft is rejected

if the underlying graph has changed since the discussion began, while suggestions that would alter graph topology

remain advisory. Before an accepted scientific change is applied, deterministic checks validate its structure, identities,

and revision context. The researcher can save the workspace as a single session containing its current hypotheses,

experiments, completed analyses, follow-up state, and discussions. Reopening the session through its URL restores

these linked elements so that she can continue working from the current validation state. THESEUS preserves this

branch-structured state across sessions, but does not maintain a complete temporal history of every prior graph

configuration or edit.

User Study

We conducted a two-phase user study comparing THESEUS with a strong linear AI baseline. Phase I was a lab study

examining how 16 researchers constructed, organized, and revised hypothesis-validation plans and inspected the

rationales connecting hypotheses and experiments. Phase II followed 10 participants for one week to examine how the

two conditions supported the interpretation of experimental results, comparison of possible next steps, and revision of
validation plans as new evidence was incorporated.

Reflecting common AI-assisted research practices reported by participants in the formative study, the baseline

combined a structured, editable document with a task-oriented AI assistant for developing and maintaining a hypothesis-

validation plan. The document was initialized with a template for organizing the plan, and participants could retain,
revise, and organize generated content throughout the study. The AI assistant provided six functions spanning the

main activities in the validation workflow: hypothesis decomposition, experiment planning, protocol generation,

result interpretation, replacement-hypothesis generation, and open-ended discussion. Both conditions used the same

underlying AI model and backend workflows and provided the same categories of AI-generated scientific content. In
the baseline, these capabilities were accessed through the editable linear document and function-specific conversations,

whereas THESEUS organized hypotheses, rationales, experiments, results, interpretations, and revisions within an

evolving branch-based system. Appendix C provides additional details about the baseline interface and individual

functions.
Our study addressed four research questions:

• RQ1: How does a branch-based structure affect researchers’ ability to explore and organize sub-hypotheses?

• RQ2: How does externalized rationale affect researchers’ understanding of why sub-hypotheses and experiments

are included?
• RQ3: How do branch-specific interpretations and alternative LLM suggestions support researchers’ next-step
decisions?
• RQ4: How does the user experience of THESEUS compare with that of the strong linear AI baseline?

5.1

Study Design

5.1.1 Participants. We recruited 16 researchers engaged in hypothesis-driven, biology-related research. All 16 completed

Phase I, and 10 opted to continue to the one-week Phase II study. The user-study sample was separate from the 10

formative-interview participants and six intermediate co-design participants. Participants were recruited through

email outreach and personal networks and were eligible if they were actively engaged in scientific research involving
hypothesis validation; Phase I was conducted in both in-person and remote settings over approximately 75 minutes,

with compensation of $40 for Phase I and $40 for Phase II. Participant-level condition order and Phase II participation

are reported in Appendix D.1.

5.1.2 Task. Participants first selected two hypothesis candidates from their own current or recent research. Before

condition assignment, the researcher and participant compared the two candidates in terms of scope, scientific maturity,

anticipated difficulty, and the amount of experimental planning required. If the two hypotheses differed substantially on
these dimensions, the participant selected or adjusted a candidate so that the pair was more comparable. This matching

process helped ensure that the two conditions were evaluated on hypotheses of comparable difficulty while allowing

participants to work on research they knew well.

One hypothesis was assigned to THESEUS and the other to the baseline, with hypothesis assignment and condition
order counterbalanced across participants. The 10 participants who continued to Phase II retained the same matched

hypothesis pair and continued working with the hypothesis previously assigned to each condition. This allowed Phase

II to examine continued interpretation and revision within the same research context across the two conditions.

5.1.3 Procedure. Phase I: controlled planning comparison. Participants first received a standardized introduction

to THESEUS and the baseline and completed a brief practice task using a hypothesis unrelated to their two study

hypotheses. Condition order was counterbalanced across participants, with half using THESEUS first and half using the
baseline first. For each condition, participants entered one of the two hypotheses they had selected from their own

research and generated an initial hypothesis-validation plan. They reviewed the resulting sub-hypotheses, rationales,

and experiment plans; identified content that was important, missing, unclear, or insufficiently justified; and edited

sub-hypotheses and experiments as needed until they were satisfied with the plan. After each condition, participants
completed a post-condition questionnaire assessing their experience with planning, rationale inspection, control,

confidence, and workload. After both conditions, we conducted a comparative semi-structured interview focused on

how THESEUS and the baseline supported hypothesis organization, rationale inspection, plan revision, and the overall

planning workflow.
Phase II: one-week field comparison. The 10 returning participants continued working on the same two hypotheses

selected and matched in Phase I and retained their Phase I condition order. They used one condition for two working

days and the other condition for two working days, with the fifth day reserved for the final comparative survey

and interview. In each condition, participants conducted experiments based on their planned protocols, recorded the
resulting observations in the provided CSV format, and uploaded two or three experimental results to the system. They

reviewed the interpretation of each result and the suggested next steps and revised the validation plan when appropriate.

After each condition, participants completed a post-condition questionnaire assessing result interpretation, next-step

support, confidence, and control. After both conditions, we conducted a final comparative survey and semi-structured

interview focused on result interpretation, replanning, and continued hypothesis-validation work.

Discussion

In this section, we reflect on our findings from the design and evaluation of THESEUS. We also discuss the broader
design implications and future opportunities for supporting human–AI collaboration in hypothesis validation.

6.1

Design Implication 1: From AI Responses to Branch-Structured Validation State

Prior work has explored hierarchical representations of hypotheses and evidence and interactive representations for
externalizing scientific reasoning [12, 29, 58]. Our findings suggest that such representations can play a broader role
during hypothesis validation. Participants used THESEUS not only to inspect generated hypotheses or experiments, but

to maintain relationships among hypotheses, rationales, experiments, results, and interpretations as their validation

plans evolved. The expert assessment similarly showed improvements in hierarchical coherence, traceability, and

testability. Together, these findings suggest that scientific AI systems should treat hypothesis validation as a branchstructured and revisable validation state, rather than as information reconstructed from a sequence of AI responses.

This matters because validation is cumulative and selective: a new result may strengthen one explanation, weaken

another, motivate a new experiment, or change how an existing result is interpreted without invalidating the rest of the

plan. When these relationships remain implicit in a conversation history, researchers must repeatedly reconstruct what
changed, what remains supported, and how earlier decisions relate to the current investigation.

Maintaining rationale within this branch-structured state also elevates it from an explanation of an individual

AI output to a traceable scientific justification. Participants valued seeing why an experiment was connected to a

particular hypothesis, while the expert assessment showed improvements in evidence–hypothesis traceability. Rather
than disappearing after an AI response is generated, rationale can remain attached to the relationship it justifies

and be revisited when new evidence changes how that relationship should be understood. This extends work on

transparent and controllable AI workflows [44, 59] from exposing individual AI operations toward maintaining the

reasons behind scientific relationships as an investigation evolves. Keeping sources, assumptions, prior evidence, and
rationales associated with these relationships can help researchers inspect not only what a validation plan contains, but
why its components are connected and how new evidence changes their interpretation.
At the same time, structured and continuous validation state should not imply correctness. A well-organized graph
can still contain weakly supported links, incorrect interpretations, unresolved assumptions, or plausible-sounding but
insufficiently grounded rationales. Systems should therefore preserve the sources and assumptions behind scientific

relationships, expose conflicting evidence and alternative interpretations, and allow rationales to be revised without

silently changing the claims they support. The goal is not simply clearer organization, but an evolving validation state

whose justification remains inspectable, contestable, and revisable. This distinction is important for scientific AI because
making reasoning visible should support scrutiny rather than turn model-generated structure into a proxy for scientific
validity.

6.2

Design Implication 2: Use Locality to Coordinate Human–AI Revision

Once hypothesis validation is represented as persistent state, a central interaction question is how researchers and

AI should coordinate changes to it. Our findings suggest that a validation branch provides a useful locality for such

coordination. Participants used branches to understand where experiments belonged, identify which part of a plan was

affected by new evidence, and revise one direction while preserving unrelated reasoning. The branch therefore served
not only as a visual grouping, but as a meaningful scope for change. This is important because new evidence often has

selective effects: an experiment may weaken one mechanism while leaving sibling hypotheses and their associated

reasoning intact.

This suggests a different model of steerability from linear conversational interaction. In a chat interface, correcting an
unsatisfactory response often requires another prompt and then checking whether accepted information also changed. In

a structured validation system, the researcher can instead indicate the particular hypothesis, experiment, interpretation,

or branch that should be reconsidered while the system uses surrounding context to support a localized revision.

Structure thus becomes more than an external representation of reasoning; it becomes an interaction mechanism
for specifying the scope of AI action. Making dependencies visible could further help researchers understand what a

proposed revision would affect before accepting it and preserve portions of the validation state that remain valid. A

related principle appears in Cobi, which recommends schedule edits and visualizes their consequences while leaving

final decisions to organizers [37]. This becomes especially important in longer-running work, where regenerating an
accepted plan can create unnecessary review effort and blur the distinction between deliberate revisions and incidental
changes in model output.
The branch should nevertheless remain a flexible coordination boundary of scientific reasoning. Real investigations
revisit parent claims, connect evidence across hypotheses, merge related explanations, and reactivate previously paused
directions. The broader design opportunity is therefore not to force scientific reasoning into a tree, but to provide

a meaningful locality within which AI suggestions can be generated, inspected, accepted, or revised while allowing

relationships to cross branch boundaries when the science requires them. In this way, structure can support selective

intervention without requiring the researcher or the system to reconsider the entire validation plan.

6.3

Design Implication 3: Separate AI Proposals from Researcher Commitments

Our findings further suggest a distinction between generating a plausible interpretation or next action and committing it

to an evolving validation plan. After reviewing experimental results, participants valued comparing possible next steps,

such as repeating or modifying an experiment, adding a control, testing another mechanism, revising a hypothesis,

or preserving an alternative direction. This reflects the method of multiple working hypotheses [9, 60]: experimental

evidence can strengthen or weaken explanations without uniquely determining what a researcher should do next.

Scientific AI should therefore avoid treating a model-generated interpretation as the automatic continuation of the

research process. AI can articulate possible interpretations and broaden the space of actions, but these outputs should

remain proposals until researchers decide what should change. This preserves room for scientific judgment when the
same result supports several plausible interpretations or follow-up strategies.

Separating proposals from commitments also allows researchers to compare alternatives in the context of the evidence

that motivated them. Interpretations and possible next steps can remain connected to the result that prompted them,

and rejected or deferred alternatives need not disappear. Because scientific conclusions are provisional, an explanation

that appears less plausible under one set of observations may become relevant again as evidence changes. Retaining

More broadly, this distinction suggests a model of human–AI collaboration in which AI expands and organizes

scientific decisions, this approach treats AI as a partner for maintaining and interrogating possible interpretations and

actions while leaving consequential research decisions under researcher control. The design challenge is therefore not

only to generate better next steps, but to make the boundary between AI suggestion and researcher commitment visible

and actionable.
6.4

Limitations and Future Work

Our study involved 16 participants in Phase I and a 10-participant subset in the one-week Phase II study, with participants
concentrated in biology-related research. While the consistency across questionnaire responses, interviews, workload
measures, and expert assessment provides evidence within this setting, longer deployments across broader scientific

domains are needed to understand how THESEUS performs as validation plans evolve over months or years and scale

to larger or collaborative projects.

Our evaluation compared THESEUS and the baseline as complete systems, so we cannot isolate the contributions
of individual components such as hierarchical organization, explicit rationale, local editing, or structured result

interpretation. Several outcomes also rely on self-report, while the blinded expert assessment involved three judges

and evaluated validation-plan quality. Future studies could use component-level ablations, larger expert panels, and

prospective evaluations of how system-supported decisions affect subsequent experiments and conclusions.
Finally, THESEUS does not fully capture practical experimental constraints and inherits limitations from its underlying

language models and retrieval systems. Although the expert evaluation showed improvements across several dimensions

of plan quality, feasibility and actionability did not reach the corrected significance threshold (𝑝 𝐻 = .052), and generated

hypotheses, rationales, experiments, or interpretations may still be incomplete or incorrect. Our evaluation examined
how these model-generated interpretations supported researchers’ validation workflows; it did not establish the

scientific accuracy of the approved/not-approved interpretation labels against independent ground truth. Work on LLM-

assisted evaluation similarly highlights the need to validate model-generated evaluators against human judgments [49].

Future work should evaluate these judgments against domain-expert assessments and examine how laboratory-specific
constraints, source reliability, and conflicting evidence can be incorporated while preserving researcher oversight.

B.2

Literature Retrieval and Use

THESEUS retrieves literature for the parent hypothesis being decomposed. An LLM generates concise search queries

based on the hypothesis’s experimental system, mechanism, variables, and relevant methods, then submits them to

Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex. Retrieved records are deduplicated using persistent identifiers and normalized titles,

then ranked using keyword overlap with the hypothesis, citation counts, abstract availability, open-access indicators,

and source-specific weights. The system prioritizes the highest-ranked papers for full-text retrieval, targeting full-text

evidence from three papers per parent hypothesis. It continues through subsequent candidates when this target is

unmet and uses available titles and abstracts when full text cannot be retrieved. Relevant passages are selected from the

available text and supplied to the decomposition agent with source references to support sub-hypothesis generation

and its accompanying rationales.

B.3

Public Protocol Retrieval and Protocol Generation

To support protocol generation, THESEUS searches protocols.io for public protocols relevant to each proposed experi-

ment. An LLM generates short queries from the experiment candidate and laboratory context, favoring established

technique names and assay terms. Retrieved records are deduplicated and ranked for relevance to the experiment.

For selected references, the system retrieves descriptions, materials, and compact previews of procedural steps when

available, retaining source titles, URLs, and DOIs. THESEUS also provides experiment-design support according to the

proposed factors and endpoints. When factorial design support is applicable, pyDOE generates a full-factorial design

matrix from the identified factors and their levels. For experiments identified as requiring statistical power analysis,

the system prepares guidance for a manual G*Power analysis, including the number of conditions, significance level,

and target power. Researchers select the statistical test and supply an effect-size estimate from prior data, literature,

or a pilot study to determine the final sample size in G*Power. Any provisional replicate count remains a planning

assumption until this analysis is completed. The protocol-writing LLM interprets the available procedural references

and design-support information alongside the proposed experiment, mapped hypotheses, method information, and

laboratory context to generate a structured protocol with materials, setup, actions, controls, measurements, recording

instructions, and expected patterns. Hypothesis-defining conditions must be preserved, while proposed defaults for

other operational details are identified as assumptions. A separate LLM reviewer checks the resulting draft and flags
issues requiring revision before laboratory use.

F.1.1

Scientific-Literature Query Planner.

You generate concise scholarly search queries for Semantic Scholar. Given a parent scientific
hypothesis, return keyword queries that improve paper discovery. Focus on experimental system,
mechanism, method, independent variable, dependent variable, and common synonyms. Do not invent
citations or paper titles. Do not use Boolean operators. Each query should be short, search-engine
friendly, and suitable for scientific literature metadata search.

F.1.2

You draft concise protocols.io search queries for public lab protocol discovery. The search endpoint
is a metadata keyword search over protocol name, description, and authors, so over-specific
experiment sentences often return no results. Given an experiment candidate and lab context, return
broad natural keyword queries that would help find concrete experimental protocols. Prefer
established protocol phrases and technique names over disease mutations, drug names, or full
hypotheses. Useful examples include cell viability assay, organoid culture, drug screening,
CellTiter-Glo, western blot, immunofluorescence staining, and qPCR gene expression. Avoid exact
quotes, Boolean operators, and fragile combinations such as organoid viability assay or phospho ERK
western blot unless the phrase is clearly a common protocol title. Do not invent protocol titles,
URLs, IDs, citations, or source claims. Keep each query under about 6 words.
